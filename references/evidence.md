# Evidence

Review agents cite this file. Four research agents built it on 2026-09-24. They opened each source, and they dropped every claim that they could not confirm. Each entry names its source and says how it was checked.

## How to use this file

- Cite an entry by its heading.
- Read the Limits line before you apply an entry. It says how far the entry transfers.
- **Kind** is one of: measured (someone counted something), postmortem (developers describe what happened), principle (a designer's rule or method), or research (a published study). Some claims also say SAID, MEASURED, or PLAYERS CLAIMED. These labels separate what a studio counted from what it said and from what players said.
- **Measured what** says whether the data measured fun or sentiment, retention or play time, or spending. Only the first one is evidence of fun.
- **Strength** (on psychology entries) says how well the finding holds up, including problems with replication.
- **Verified** says whether the researcher opened the primary source or used a secondary report.
- Paraphrases are marked. Words in quotation marks were confirmed in the source.
- Read "Myths and misuses" before you cite a famous story.
- Sources can move or change. If a finding depends on one entry, open its source again.
- To use new evidence in a review, open the source first. Add it to this file only after the same checks.

## Designer principles

### Fun is learning, and mastered patterns become boring (Koster)
- Claim: Fun comes from learning and mastering patterns, so a game drifts toward boredom once the player has mastered its patterns. Koster's 2025 restatement: "fun is basically about making progress on prediction," and designers should "escalate the situations so that theories can be tested, refined, and abandoned."
- Kind: principle
- Source: A Theory of Fun for Game Design, Raph Koster (original edition, then a revised 2nd edition from O'Reilly), https://www.theoryoffun.com/ and "Game design is simple, actually", Raph Koster, 2025, https://www.raphkoster.com/2025/11/03/game-design-is-simple-actually/
- Verified: fetched primary (2025 post). The book line "the destiny of games is to become boring, not to be fun" is confirmed as a quote in Soren Johnson's column "Water Finds a Crack" (secondary, designer-notes.com).
- Review question: What does the player still have to learn from this system after 1 hour, 10 hours, and 50 hours? What new situation forces them to revise a strategy that already works?
- Use it for: MASTERY, MOTIVATION
- Limits: This is a lens for challenge-based fun. Other kinds of fun (Lazzaro's Easy Fun and People Fun, MDA's Fellowship and Submission) do not depend on learning. The 2025 post also says a problem that is too simple or already mastered leaves players bored or stuck in one strategy.

### Players automate the tedious parts (Koster's Laws)
- Claim: "No matter what you do, someone is going to automate the process of playing your world." Corollary: "Looking at what parts of your game players tend to automate is a good way to determine which parts of the game are tedious and/or not fun."
- Kind: principle
- Source: The Laws of Online World Design, collected by Raph Koster (first posted 1998, page updated 2023), https://www.raphkoster.com/games/laws-of-online-world-design/the-laws-of-online-world-design/
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: If a player could write a macro for this system, which part would they automate first? Why does that part exist?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, UX
- Limits: The law comes from online worlds, where bots have economic value. In a single-player or co-op game, use it as a thought test ("what would players want to skip?"), not as a prediction of bots. The same page holds laws from other contributors, so cite this law by its heading ("Macroing, botting, and automation").

### A game is a series of interesting decisions (Meier)
- Claim: A choice is not interesting if players always pick the same option (then the computer should pick it) or pick at random. Interesting choices have a trade-off, depend on the game situation, let players express a play style, and have a persistence the player understands. Sid's Rules adds: "Choice is only interesting when it is both impactful and informed."
- Kind: principle
- Source: "Interesting Decisions", Sid Meier (Firaxis), GDC 2012, https://gdcvault.com/play/1015756/Interesting (video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WggIdtrqgKg) and "GD Column 5: Sid's Rules", Soren Johnson, Game Developer magazine, January 2009, https://www.designer-notes.com/game-developer-column-5-sids-rules/
- Verified: fetched primary (GDC Vault session page, YouTube auto-caption transcript of the talk, and the Designer Notes column). Also secondary (Game Developer report by Leigh Alexander, March 7 2012).
- Review question: For each choice in this system, does the best option change with the situation? At the moment of choice, does the player have the information to see the trade-off?
- Use it for: DECISIONS, UX
- Limits: In the talk Meier says he does not claim the idea applies to every game, and he names rhythm games as a weak fit (paraphrase of auto-captions). He warns that something interesting to do once may not be interesting to do ten times. He suggests about three to five options as a comfortable number and warns that early choices easily overwhelm players (paraphrase of auto-captions).

### Double it or cut it in half (Meier)
- Claim: When a tuning value feels wrong, make a big change, not a small one. If a unit seems too weak, double its strength instead of cutting its cost by 5 percent. If players feel overwhelmed by upgrades, remove half of them (paraphrase). The goal is "to stake out more design territory with each successive iteration."
- Kind: principle
- Source: "GD Column 5: Sid's Rules", Soren Johnson, Game Developer magazine, January 2009, https://www.designer-notes.com/game-developer-column-5-sids-rules/
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: Does the tuning plan test large jumps (times 2, times 0.5) to find the right range first, or does it plan only small nudges?
- Use it for: DECISIONS, MASTERY
- Limits: It is a method to explore, not a rule for final values. Johnson writes: "The point is not that the new values are likely to be correct." It fits prototype and playtest loops, where the number of iterations is limited.

### One good game is better than two great ones (the Covert Action rule)
- Claim: Two sub-games that are each fun can ruin each other when they compete for the player's attention. In Johnson's words, "an idea may be 'fun' on its own but still not make the game better if it distracts the player from the target experience."
- Kind: postmortem
- Source: "GD Column 5: Sid's Rules", Soren Johnson, Game Developer magazine, January 2009, https://www.designer-notes.com/game-developer-column-5-sids-rules/
- Verified: fetched primary (includes Meier's own account of Covert Action)
- Review question: Does this new system feed the core loop, or does it pull the player's attention away from the core loop for long stretches?
- Use it for: FANTASY, UX
- Limits: Mixes work when one part is the clear focus and the other parts are short or serve it. The column cites Pirates! (short mini-games) and X-Com (the strategy layer exists to make the tactical battles matter).

### Players will optimize the fun out of a game (Johnson)
- Claim: "Given the opportunity, players will optimize the fun out of a game." A dominant strategy "actually takes away choice from a game," and a trickle of safe reward makes players trade time for safety until the game "begins to feel like a grind."
- Kind: postmortem
- Source: "GD Column 17: Water Finds a Crack", Soren Johnson, Game Developer magazine, March 2011 (posted June 12 2011), https://www.designer-notes.com/game-developer-column-17-water-finds-a-crack/
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: What is the safest and most repeatable way to get this system's reward? Is that way fun? If not, what cost or rule stops it from dominating?
- Use it for: DECISIONS, MOTIVATION
- Limits: Johnson also warns that "designers can go too far by trying to remove all exploits." Some exploits are fun shortcuts. His fix for reload abuse in Civ 3 was a game-start option, and he ends: "the designer can't go wrong putting the player in control of his or her own experience." He names the target: "a specific decision is right in some circumstances but not in others."

### Fighting against human nature is a losing battle (Rosewater, lesson 1)
- Claim: Change the game to match what players instinctively do. In Time Spiral, playtesters kept trying to attack with suspended creatures, so the team gave all suspended creatures haste. "Don't get yourself into a fight you're probably not going to win."
- Kind: postmortem
- Source: "Twenty Years, Twenty Lessons, Part 1", Mark Rosewater, Making Magic, 2016, https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/twenty-years-twenty-lessons-part-1-2016-05-30 (talk: GDC 2016, https://gdcvault.com/play/1023186/Twenty-Years-Twenty)
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: In playtests, what do players try to do with this system that the rules forbid? Can the rules allow it instead?
- Use it for: UX, MASTERY
- Limits: Rosewater grants that some products do change behavior (he cites the cell phone), but says not to assume your game is one. Separate "players want to do X" from "X would remove the challenge."

### Don't confuse "interesting" with "fun" (Rosewater, lesson 5)
- Claim: Intellectual stimulation is not the same as emotional stimulation, and emotional satisfaction usually runs deeper. Odyssey's threshold made discarding your hand the correct play, but players "didn't want to throw away their whole hand. They wanted to play their cards."
- Kind: postmortem
- Source: "Twenty Years, Twenty Lessons, Part 1", Mark Rosewater, Making Magic, 2016, https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/twenty-years-twenty-lessons-part-1-2016-05-30
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: Is this mechanic exciting to play, or is it only clever to read about in the doc?
- Use it for: FANTASY, DECISIONS
- Limits: Rosewater says both kinds of stimulation are valuable. His R&D test is a question to ask, "Is this interesting or is this fun?", not a ban on clever mechanics.

### Make the fun part also the correct strategy to win (Rosewater, lesson 13)
- Claim: "It's not the players' job to find the fun. It is your job as the game designer to put the fun where the players can't help but find it." Unhinged's gotcha cards made the winning play to stop talking, moving, and laughing.
- Kind: postmortem
- Source: "Twenty Years, Twenty Lessons, Part 2", Mark Rosewater, Making Magic, 2016, https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/twenty-years-twenty-lessons-part-2-2016-06-06
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: If a player plays this system in the most efficient way, is that also the most fun way?
- Use it for: DECISIONS, MOTIVATION
- Limits: This lesson states the goal. Johnson's "Water Finds a Crack" gives the tools (scale costs, remove dominant strategies, or add options). Players will still search for degenerate lines.

### Lenticular design (Rosewater)
- Claim: Some designs look simple to a beginner and deep to an expert, because the depth needs knowledge to see. The motive is "to add complexity to enhance the game for the more-experienced players without increasing the difficulty for the less-experienced ones."
- Kind: principle
- Source: "Lenticular Design", Mark Rosewater, Making Magic, March 31 2014, https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/lenticular-design-2014-03-31
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: What does a new player see in this element on day one? What extra depth does an expert see in the same element?
- Use it for: MASTERY, UX
- Limits: Rosewater separates comprehension complexity, board complexity, and strategic complexity. New players notice comprehension complexity at once. Only strategic complexity hides well, so dense rules text or UI still costs new players.

### Schell's lenses
- Claim: Schell's book gives sets of questions ("lenses") to examine a design from one angle at a time. Four fit a system review: Essential Experience ("What experience do I want the player to have?"), Meaningful Choice ("Are there any dominant strategies in my game?"), Flow ("Does my game provide a steady stream of gradually increasing challenges?"), and Triangularity ("are the rewards commensurate with the risks?").
- Kind: principle
- Source: The Art of Game Design: A Book of Lenses, Jesse Schell, 1st edition 2008 (Morgan Kaufmann), 3rd edition 2019 (CRC Press), https://schellgames.com/art-of-game-design
- Verified: secondary (lens card text transcribed at https://gamefroot-developer.gitbooks.io/jesse-schell-the-art-of-game-design-lenses-1-to-1/content/ with first-edition numbering, edition data from publisher listings)
- Review question: What is the essential experience? Is there a dominant strategy? Does challenge rise steadily? Is there a safe option with a small reward next to a risky option with a big reward?
- Use it for: DECISIONS, MASTERY, FANTASY
- Limits: The lenses are questions, not answers. Lens numbers and some wording differ between editions, so cite a lens by name, not by number.

### MDA and the eight kinds of fun (Hunicke, LeBlanc, Zubek)
- Claim: Designers build Mechanics, which produce Dynamics ("the run-time behavior of the mechanics"), which produce Aesthetics ("the desirable emotional responses evoked in the player"). Players meet the game in the reverse order. The paper's taxonomy: Sensation, Fantasy, Narrative, Challenge, Fellowship, Discovery, Expression, Submission.
- Kind: principle
- Source: "MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game Research", Robin Hunicke, Marc LeBlanc, Robert Zubek, 2004, https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/MDA.pdf
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: Which two or three of the eight aesthetics does this system serve? Which dynamic produces each one, and which mechanic produces that dynamic?
- Use it for: FANTASY, SOCIAL, MOTIVATION
- Limits: The paper says its list is "not limited to" these eight and that there is no formula for the right mix. Frank Lantz (2015, https://gamedesignadvance.com/?p=2995) says the terms mislead readers: people read "aesthetics" as visuals and "mechanics" as rules only.

### Four Keys to Fun (Lazzaro)
- Claim: XEODesign watched people play their favorite games and grouped the emotions into four keys: Hard Fun (fiero, triumph over a challenge), Easy Fun (curiosity), Serious Fun (relaxation and excitement), and People Fun (amusement from competition and cooperation). Lazzaro reports that best-selling games offer at least three of the four.
- Kind: research
- Source: "Why We Play Games: Four Keys to More Emotion Without Story", Nicole Lazzaro, XEODesign, 2004 (GDC 2004 talk), https://www.nicolelazzaro.com/the4-keys-to-fun/ (talk text: https://archive.org/stream/GDC2004Lazzaro/GDC2004-Lazzaro_djvu.txt)
- Verified: fetched primary (Lazzaro's site and the GDC 2004 text on archive.org). Also secondary (Game Developer report by Brad Kane, March 26 2004).
- Review question: Which key does this system feed? With this system added, does the game still cover at least three keys?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, SOCIAL, FANTASY
- Limits: The study is qualitative observation. The "at least three keys" finding comes from analysis of hit games, not from a controlled test. The key names changed between versions (see Myths).

### Flow and player-driven difficulty (Chen)
- Claim: Chen treats fun as flow, a balance between challenge and ability. "Like fingerprints, different people have different skills and Flow Zones," so a game should offer a wide range of challenge and "embed DDA choices into the core gameplay mechanics and let player make their choices through play."
- Kind: research
- Source: "Flow in Games", Jenova Chen, MFA thesis (USC), 2006, https://www.jenovachen.com/flowingames/Flow_in_games_final.pdf
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: How do a weak player and a strong player each find the right challenge in this system? Do they do it through play, or only through a menu?
- Use it for: MASTERY, UX
- Limits: Chen lists Csikszentmihalyi's eight flow components and notes that not all are needed for flow. He argues against passive, performance-based difficulty adjustment because performance data misreads players (a player who does suicidal stunts in GTA for fun is not a weak player). Flow describes moment-to-moment play, so it says little about long-term goals.

### Skill atoms and burnout (Cook)
- Claim: Each skill a player learns is a loop of action, simulation, feedback, and modeling. When players master a skill "but fail to find any interesting use for it. This is known as burnout." Early burnout "can chop off huge sections of the player's potential experience."
- Kind: principle
- Source: "The Chemistry of Game Design", Daniel Cook, Gamasutra (now Game Developer), July 19 2007, https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-chemistry-of-game-design (blog: https://lostgarden.com/2007/07/19/the-chemistry-of-game-design/)
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: For each new verb, item, or stat in this system, where does the player first get feedback that it matters? Which later skill does it unlock?
- Use it for: MASTERY, UX
- Limits: Skill chains model learning well but model social or expressive play poorly. A chain on paper does not prove that players find each link, so the review still needs playtest evidence.

### Loops and arcs (Cook)
- Claim: Loops are interactions the player repeats while updating a mental model. An arc is "a broken loop you exit immediately," such as a story beat. People "burn out on them rapidly, rarely desiring to experience them more than once."
- Kind: principle
- Source: "Loops and Arcs", Daniel Cook, Lostgarden, April 30 2012, https://lostgarden.com/2012/04/30/loops-and-arcs/
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: Which parts of this system are loops that stay interesting on repeat, and which are arcs that are spent after one use? Does the content budget match that split?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, MASTERY
- Limits: Cook values arcs for authored meaning. Arcs are not bad, but each one is used up after one use. His advice "Understand what is filler" needs the design goals to decide what counts as filler.

### The Player Model Principle (Sylvester)
- Claim: "The whole value of a game is in the mental model of itself it projects into the player's mind." So "anything in the Game Model that doesn't copy into the Player Model is worthless." Simple, readable cues let apophenia make players imagine richer stories than the code holds.
- Kind: principle
- Source: "The Simulation Dream", Tynan Sylvester, 2013 (talk at the Ottawa International Game Conference, June 1 2013), https://tynansylvester.com/2013/06/the-simulation-dream/ . Related: Designing Games (O'Reilly, 2013), which seeks solutions with "the lowest cost in player attention and developer work, and the highest benefit in emotional punch," https://tynansylvester.com/book/
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: Which rules in this system will the player actually notice and understand? Which hidden rules should the design cut or make visible?
- Use it for: UX, FANTASY
- Limits: Sylvester's examples come from simulation games (the Ultima Online and BioShock ecologies). Players who read wikis or data-mine can bring hidden rules into their model later, so what counts as "hidden" depends on the audience.

### Risk and reward (Sakurai)
- Claim: Sakurai defines gameplay as the interplay of risk and return. The best reward should sit right where risk peaks, as when Mario stomps an enemy at the moment of greatest danger. He argues that a flashy upgrade such as a three-way shot in Space Invaders can be poor design because it removes the risk the game depends on (paraphrase).
- Kind: principle
- Source: "Risk and Reward [Game Essence]", Masahiro Sakurai on Creating Games (YouTube), 2022, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FXqEykD5Ub4
- Verified: secondary (Nintendo Life, August 30 2022). Details also confirmed in the English auto-captions of Sakurai's Japanese compilation video https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i7WJTzwEuFI (machine translation, so paraphrase only).
- Review question: Where does risk peak in this system, and is the best reward placed there? Does any upgrade remove the risk that made the reward feel earned?
- Use it for: DECISIONS, MASTERY
- Limits: Sakurai's examples are action games. He notes the three-way shot could be fine if enemies also fired diagonally or ammo were limited, so judge an upgrade in its context.

### Hitstop sells impact (Sakurai)
- Claim: When a strike lands, "both parties momentarily freeze, emphasizing the power of impact." Sakurai varies the freeze by attack strength and type, gives projectiles less, and caps it so multiplayer does not slow down too much.
- Kind: principle
- Source: "Thinking About Hitstop", Masahiro Sakurai, Weekly Famitsu column (vol. 490-491), English translation by Source Gaming, November 11 2015, https://sourcegaming.info/2015/11/11/thoughts-on-hitstop-sakurais-famitsu-column-vol-490-1/
- Verified: secondary (Source Gaming translation). His episode "Stop for Big Moments! [Design Specifics]" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OdVkEOzdCPw) covers the same topic. Only its title was checked, not its content.
- Review question: When this system's key event happens (a hit, a kill, a rare drop), what feedback tells the player it mattered? Does the feedback scale with the size of the event?
- Use it for: UX, FANTASY
- Limits: Hitstop costs time. Too much slows the game, which is why Sakurai caps it. It suits melee impact more than projectiles.

### Trap options and viability (Sawyer)
- Claim: "Balance in a single-player CRPG is important to the extent that it allows players making different character and gear choices to be viable through the content of the game." Sawyer criticizes systems with "a boatload of trap choices," where "The dividing factor was system mastery." An overpowered outlier "becomes an unlabeled Easy difficulty slider rendering all other options/choices irrelevant."
- Kind: principle
- Source: "Balance in Single-Player CRPGs", Josh Sawyer, Tumblr, 2017 (his linking tweet is dated June 1 2017), https://www.tumblr.com/jesawyer/161302725596/balance-in-single-player-crpgs
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: Can a player who picks the "wrong" option still finish the content? Can a new player tell which options are weak before they commit?
- Use it for: DECISIONS, MASTERY
- Limits: Sawyer's goal is viability, not equal power. Johnson's "Good Exploits?" section notes that some standout strategies are fun if they do not drown out all others.

### Thirty seconds of fun, in nested loops (Griesemer)
- Claim: Griesemer says the real point of the Halo quote was "a 3-second loop inside of a 30-second loop inside of a 3-minute loop that is always different." The 30 seconds are replayed "in different environments, with different weapons, different vehicles, against different enemies."
- Kind: postmortem
- Source: "Half-Minute Halo: An Interview with Jaime Griesemer", Ludwig Kietzmann, Engadget, July 14 2011, https://www.engadget.com/2011-07-14-half-minute-halo-an-interview-with-jaime-griesemer.html
- Verified: fetched primary (interview)
- Review question: What is this system's short loop? What middle loop changes its context? What long loop makes each session feel different?
- Use it for: MASTERY, MOTIVATION
- Limits: The time scales come from Halo's combat and AI work (a 2002 GDC talk with Chris Butcher). Other genres need their own time scales. Griesemer credits much of Bungie's design wisdom to Jason Jones.

### Overlapping axes of randomness (Wilson, Path of Exile)
- Claim: Content that players repeat hundreds of times needs several independent random axes, not a longer list of hand-made variants. In Breach, location, monster density, rare chests, breach type, and bosses all vary, so players never see a perfect one and expect the next to be better (paraphrase).
- Kind: postmortem
- Source: "Designing Path of Exile to Be Played Forever", Chris Wilson (Grinding Gear Games), GDC 2019, https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1025784/Designing-Path-of-Exile-to (video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tmuy9fyNUjY)
- Verified: fetched primary (YouTube auto-caption transcript, so paraphrase only). Session description confirmed on gdconf.com.
- Review question: How many independent axes vary in this system's content? After 50 repeats, what can still surprise the player?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, MASTERY
- Limits: Wilson frames this inside five pillars: visceral combat, random levels, random items, a secure economy with trade, and deep character customization. He argues items need trade to have worth, which fits an online economy and may not transfer to a small co-op game. He also defends hard endgame content that "maybe 10%" of players reach, because top players want exclusive challenges and streamers show that content to everyone else (auto-captions, paraphrase).

### Teach by play (Miyamoto, World 1-1)
- Claim: Miyamoto says World 1-1 was built so the player can "gradually and naturally understand what they're doing," then "start to play more freely." Nintendo aims for the point where "it becomes their game."
- Kind: postmortem
- Source: Eurogamer video interview with Shigeru Miyamoto, 2015, as reported in "How Miyamoto built Super Mario Bros.' legendary World 1-1", Chris Kerr, Game Developer, September 8 2015, https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-miyamoto-built-i-super-mario-bros-i-legendary-world-1-1
- Verified: secondary (Game Developer)
- Review question: How does the player meet this system in a safe first encounter, and learn it without a text tutorial, before the game tests them on it?
- Use it for: UX, MASTERY
- Limits: World 1-1 teaches a few verbs in a linear level. Systems with many options (builds, crafting) often need explicit information too, as Meier's "impactful and informed" rule shows.

### Luck and skill are not opposites (Garfield)
- Claim: Luck and skill are separate features of a game, not two ends of one scale. In Garfield's "rando chess," the result flips after the game with some probability (1/6 in the blog's account). The game has more luck but exactly the same skill. He defines luck as uncertainty in outcome, which includes opponents' hidden choices and imperfect human execution.
- Kind: principle
- Source: "Dr. Richard Garfield on 'Luck Versus Skill'", Magic Cruise 2012, video by TCGplayer, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dSg408i-eKw
- Verified: secondary ("20 thoughts on skill vs. chance in poker, part 2", Mike Stein, Quantitative Poker, September 2012, http://www.quantitativepoker.com/2012/09/20-thoughts-on-skill-vs-chance-part-2.html). I did not watch the video.
- Review question: Where does this system's uncertainty come from (dice, hidden information, other players, execution)? Does added randomness remove skill, or does it only add variance?
- Use it for: DECISIONS, MASTERY, SOCIAL
- Limits: Players judge randomness by feel, not math. In his GDC 2012 talk, Meier says Civilization Revolution adjusted die rolls so that too many bad results did not happen in a row (auto-captions, paraphrase). More luck also means more games before skill shows in results.

### Take the sting out of failure (Kasavin, Hades)
- Claim: "It was an explicit goal of our early development, to take the pain out of dying and having to restart." Each death gives story and progress, so players feel the time "wasn't a waste of your time." God Mode starts at 20 percent damage resistance and adds 2 percent per death, up to 80 percent (per Inverse).
- Kind: postmortem
- Source: "How Supergiant weaves narrative rewards into Hades' cycle of perpetual death", Alissa McAloon, Game Developer, February 13 2020 (quoting a Rock Paper Shotgun interview), https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-supergiant-weaves-narrative-rewards-into-i-hades-i-cycle-of-perpetual-death and "Hades devs reveal how God Mode solves the worst thing about the genre", Tomas Franzese, Inverse, 2021, https://www.inverse.com/gaming/hades-god-mode-interview
- Verified: secondary (Game Developer, Inverse)
- Review question: When the player fails in this system, what do they keep, and what new thing do they see, so that the next attempt feels like progress?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, FANTASY
- Limits: Kasavin also says that if players "could just blow through it, what's interesting about the game goes away," because dying and looping is part of the experience. He says difficulty options "may need to be proprietary to the game." Softer failure must not remove the challenge that the loop depends on.

## Live games and measured data

### WoW rest system: a bonus, not a penalty
- Claim: SAID (Rob Pardo, GDC 2010, as reported by Shacknews): in beta, XP ran at 100% and then fell to 50% after long sessions, and the system "was universally hated among the testers." Blizzard doubled the XP needed per level and gave 200% XP after rest that decays to 100%. The math stayed the same, but players read it as a reward. No survey data is given. The evidence is tester reaction.
- Kind: postmortem
- Measured what: none (statement only)
- Source: "Sid Meier and Rob Pardo on Probability and Player Psychology", Brian Leahy, Shacknews, 2010, https://www.shacknews.com/article/62807/sid-meier-and-rob-pardo . Also "Framing and World of Warcraft's Rest System", Jamie Madigan, The Psychology of Games, 2010, https://www.psychologyofgames.com/2010/03/framing-and-world-of-warcrafts-rest-system/ (relays the story through Chris Remo on the Idle Thumbs podcast).
- Verified: secondary (Shacknews report of Pardo's GDC 2010 talk). I found no Blizzard primary source (no slides, video, or Blizzard post). Madigan's post is a second, weaker chain (Remo on a podcast).
- Review question: Does any limit in this system read as a penalty for playing, when the same limit could read as a bonus for coming back?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, UX
- Limits: Framing changes the feeling, not the pacing. Min-max players still compute the real rate. The only numbers are Pardo's example percentages, from one reported talk.

### Diablo 3 auction houses: trade short-circuits the loot loop
- Claim: MEASURED (Jay Wilson, GDC 2013): Blizzard expected only a small share of players to use the auction houses. Nearly all players used one or both, and over 50% used them regularly. SAID: the houses "really hurt the game", the gold house did "much more damage" than the real-money house, and money became a bigger motive than killing Diablo. Blizzard removed both on 18 March 2014 because they "undermine Diablo's core game play: kill monsters to get cool loot." Josh Mosqueira later said the team had expected "only about 10% of players" to use it.
- Kind: postmortem
- Measured what: retention or play time (here: the share of players who used the feature)
- Source: "Diablo III Auction House Update", Blizzard Entertainment, 2013, https://news.blizzard.com/en-gb/article/10974978/diablo-iii-auction-house-update . "Diablo 3 Director Jay Wilson: Auction Houses 'really hurt' game", Mike Schramm, Engadget, 2013, https://www.engadget.com/2013-03-28-diablo-3-director-jay-wilson-auction-houses-really-hurt-game.html . "Against the Burning Hells: Diablo III's Road to Redemption with Reaper of Souls", Josh Mosqueira, GDC 2015, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vWYEWRrFgUY
- Verified: fetched primary (Blizzard post, GDC 2015 talk transcript). Wilson quotes: secondary (Engadget, MCV/DEVELOP).
- Review question: Can a player get this system's best reward without doing the core activity (a shop, trade, a vendor, gifting, or a crafting shortcut)? Does that path beat the core loop on reward per minute? If yes, what will most players do?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, DECISIONS, SOCIAL
- Limits: Loot games where the drop is the reward. Mosqueira said a game "all about social trading" is a different case. Blizzard's "small share" guess failed at scale, so small games may see a thinner market. A game with no market can still bypass its drop loop through a vendor, gifting, or a crafting shortcut.

### Diablo 3 Loot 2.0: fewer, better, readable drops
- Claim: SAID (Mosqueira, GDC 2015): at launch the game had "way too many items", players "didn't really understand the concept of rarity versus power", and legendaries were "piles of stats" instead of build changers. He said his own character took "104 hours before I found my first legendary." He tells of a 45-minute designer debate over which of two items was better. If designers could not agree, players had "no hope". Loot 2.0 dropped fewer and better items, added smart drops for the player's class, and aimed for build-changing legendaries. Reaper of Souls also "made variety the most efficient way of playing the game" (Adventure Mode).
- Kind: postmortem
- Measured what: none (statement only)
- Source: "Against the Burning Hells: Diablo III's Road to Redemption with Reaper of Souls", Josh Mosqueira, GDC 2015, https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1021776/Against-the-Burning-Hells-Diablo (video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vWYEWRrFgUY) . Smart drops: "2.0.1 PTR and Closed Beta Patch Notes", Nevalistis, Blizzard Entertainment, 2013, http://web.archive.org/web/20140101173655/http://us.battle.net/d3/en/blog/11883960/ . The 104-hour quote: "Diablo 3's Josh Mosqueira on launch failures, mistakes, and the road to redemption", PCGamesN, 2015, https://www.pcgamesn.com/diablo-iii/diablo-3s-josh-mosqueria-on-launch-failures-mistakes-and-the-road-to-redemption
- Verified: fetched primary (talk transcript)
- Review question: When a reward drops, can the player tell in a few seconds if it is an upgrade and why it matters to the build? Is the most efficient play pattern also a varied one?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, UX, DECISIONS, FANTASY
- Limits: ARPG loot with many drops per minute. Games with few hand-placed rewards have a different problem. The talk shows no metrics.

### Chris Wilson: players find problems, not solutions
- Claim: SAID (Wilson, video): do not survey players to set design direction. Surveys create expectations, over-weight the loudest players, and pull toward "immediate comfort: more rewards, less friction, less punishment, more power, more convenience." He says to watch play, ask about specific pain points, and "Ask them whether they're having the experience that you intended." He opens with Mark Rosewater: "Your audience is good at recognising problems and bad at solving them."
- Kind: principle
- Measured what: none (statement only)
- Source: "Don't survey your players" (YouTube video), Chris Wilson, upload year not confirmed (reported by FRVR on 28 May 2026), https://youtu.be/J91NYC31yKU
- Verified: fetched primary (video transcript). Coverage: secondary (FRVR, GamesRadar+).
- Review question: Does the doc treat player requests as the design, or as evidence of a problem? What intended experience will the team check the system against?
- Use it for: DECISIONS, MOTIVATION
- Limits: One designer's opinion, not data. Old School RuneScape gives players a veto and thrives, so the rule depends on the product.

### Old School RuneScape: player polls as a veto
- Claim: MEASURED and SAID (Jagex, 2022): fewer than 6% of active members vote in polls. With a 75% pass mark, 1.5% of active members voting no can block content. Jagex cut the mark to 70% because updates that fell "just barely short" meant many players missed content they voted for. Balance and integrity fixes are not polled. The 2013 launch poll promised "regular members' polls" only at the 750,000-vote tier. The poll got 449,351 votes, and Jagex added polls anyway.
- Kind: measured
- Measured what: fun or sentiment (stated preference of voters) and turnout
- Source: "Polling Changes", Jagex, 2022, https://secure.runescape.com/m=news/polling-changes?oldschool=1 . "2007 - Old School RuneScape... You Vote!", Mark Gerhard, Jagex, 2013, https://secure.runescape.com/m=news/a=427/2007---old-school-runescape-you-vote
- Verified: fetched primary. Vote total and adoption of polls: secondary (Wikipedia, OSRS Wiki).
- Review question: If players could vote on this system, would it pass? Who votes, and whom does the vote leave out?
- Use it for: DECISIONS, SOCIAL
- Limits: A nostalgia product with an audience that wants a veto. With 6% turnout, polls show engaged players, not all players. A poll asks "do you want X", not "will X still be fun after a month". I found no source for why Jagex first chose 75%.

- Limits: A near 50/50 split after a big change can hide two strong camps. Two modes double balance work. The survey measured preference, not play time.

- Verified: secondary (Tech Times, citing Kotaku)
- Review question: Is there a spot where low effort feels like high reward, even if the math says it is not? How will other players feel when they see it?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, SOCIAL
- Limits: Shared world where players see each other farm. The source is a press report of a talk, not a transcript.

### Destiny 2 gear sunsetting and its reversal
- Claim: SAID (Bungie, Joe Blackburn, Feb 2021): "we don't believe that infusion caps are the right answer." Bungie wants a system "that doesn't make our rewards feel like they have an expiration date" and says "there's nothing more important in Destiny than getting our rewards right." Gear that could reach max Power keeps that ability permanently.
- Kind: postmortem
- Measured what: none (statement only)
- Source: "Destiny 2021 Update: The Road to The Witch Queen", Joe Blackburn, Bungie, 2021, https://www.bungie.net/7/en-us/News/article/50124 . "Destiny 2 Is Sunsetting Sunsetting", Ethan Gach, Kotaku, 2021, https://kotaku.com/destiny-2-is-ditching-sunsetting-1846356691

- Review question: Does any reward lose value on a schedule the player did not choose? What does that do to the wish to chase it?

- Limits: Seasonal live game with power creep. Designers still need a way to refresh the meta, and this post names the goal but not the replacement.

### Slay the Spire: metrics with judgment
- Claim: MEASURED: Mega Crit logged every run (cards, relics, events, choices, run length) and collected 18,168 feedback items in Discord. SAID: "Data is evidence, but not a conclusion." The card Madness showed a "grossly disproportionate" rate in winning decks only because players got it late, just before the boss. Two "super playtesters" dominated early averages. Slow healing loops were "optimal but it's really really boring", so the team "wanted to nuke" them. Ascension levels let them split data by skill.
- Kind: measured
- Measured what: none of the listed types. It measured win and pick rates (balance) plus written feedback.
- Source: "'Slay the Spire': Metrics Driven Design and Balance", Anthony Giovannetti, GDC 2019, https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1025731/-Slay-the-Spire-Metrics (slides: https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2019/presentations/Giovannetti_Anthony_SlayTheSpire.pdf , video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7rqfbvnO_H0)
- Verified: fetched primary (slides and talk transcript)
- Review question: Which metric will the team watch for this system, and what selection effect could fake it? Is any optimal strategy also boring?
- Use it for: DECISIONS, MASTERY
- Limits: Single-player roguelike, so a dominant combo only hurts the player who finds it. In PvP or co-op, the same combo affects other players.

### Left 4 Dead AI Director: pacing, not difficulty
- Claim: SAID (Michael Booth, 2009): the Director estimates each survivor's "intensity" from damage, incapacitation, ledge pulls, and nearby kills. It adds threats until intensity peaks, then removes major threats for a "Relax" period. "Algorithm adjusts pacing, not difficulty." The reasons: "Constant, unchanging combat is fatiguing" and "Long periods of inactivity are boring." Static enemy placement "Results in 'optimal strategy' that works every time" and "Kills cooperation and degenerates into a race".
- Kind: principle
- Measured what: none (statement only)
- Source: "The AI Systems of Left 4 Dead", Michael Booth, Valve, 2009, https://steamcdn-a.akamaihd.net/apps/valve/2009/ai_systems_of_l4d_mike_booth.pdf
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: Does the system create peaks and valleys, and does it know when players need a rest? Could players memorize it into one optimal route?
- Use it for: MASTERY, SOCIAL, MOTIVATION
- Limits: Co-op action in short sessions. Booth calls the intensity estimate "crude, yet the resulting pacing works". That is Valve's judgment, not a published test.

### Valve biofeedback experiment
- Claim: RESEARCH (Mike Ambinder, GDC 2011): Valve replaced the Director's estimated intensity with measured skin conductance in Left 4 Dead 2 playtests. Players rated enjoyment, frustration, and challenge on 9-point scales. The result slide says "Measured arousal produces greater enjoyment than estimated arousal." The slides also say "More work needed to 'quantify' enjoyment" and give no sample size or effect size. An Alien Swarm test that tied a timer to arousal found a "Positive feedback loop".
- Kind: research
- Measured what: fun or sentiment (post-session ratings) and physiology
- Source: "Biofeedback in Gameplay: How Valve Measures Physiology to Enhance Gaming Experience", Mike Ambinder, GDC 2011, https://cdn.cloudflare.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2011/ValveBiofeedback-Ambinder.pdf (session page: https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1014734/Biofeedback-in-Gameplay-How-Valve)
- Verified: fetched primary (slides)
- Review question: Does the system assume the game knows how the player feels? How good is that estimate, and what happens when it is wrong?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, UX
- Limits: Lab test with no reported sample size. It needs hardware that shipped games do not use. Treat it as a direction, not proof.

### EVE Online new players: test the story with data
- Claim: MEASURED (CCP Rise, Fanfest 2015): CCP took 80,000 new users and checked deaths in their first 15 days. 85% did not die, 13.5% died "legally" (for example in wars), and 1% were ganked. The ganked group was the most likely to stay subscribed, and the group that never died was the most likely to leave. Under 1% of cancellations cited ship loss or harassment. SAID: once new players engage socially, "they play more per day, they are more likely to stay subscribed." An A/B test of the new tutorial gave about a 14% rise in conversion to paid. A help overlay that staff loved was used by nobody in playtests.
- Kind: measured
- Measured what: retention or play time, spending (conversion)
- Source: "EVE Fanfest 2015: Using Science to Help Newbros", CCP Rise, CCP Games, 2015, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A92Ge2S8M1Y
- Verified: fetched primary (talk transcript)
- Review question: Which belief about player pain does the doc assume without data? What is the cheapest test that could prove it wrong?
- Use it for: SOCIAL, DECISIONS, UX
- Limits: The gank result is a correlation. Players who fly into risk are also more engaged, so death may mark engagement, not cause it. EVE's audience accepts harsh loss. Retention and conversion are not fun.

### Nick Yee: player motivations in online games
- Claim: RESEARCH: a factor analysis of 40 survey items from 3,000 MMORPG players (EverQuest, Dark Age of Camelot, Ultima Online, Star Wars Galaxies) found 10 motivation subcomponents in 3 groups: Achievement, Social, Immersion. The groups barely correlate (r < .10), so one motive does not suppress another, as Bartle assumed. Escapism best predicted problematic use (b = .31), then hours per week (b = .30).
- Kind: research
- Measured what: fun or sentiment (self-reported motives)
- Source: "Motivations for Play in Online Games", Nick Yee, CyberPsychology & Behavior 9(6), 2006, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1089/cpb.2006.9.772 (author copy: https://nickyee.com/pubs/Yee%20-%20Motivations%20(2007).pdf)
- Verified: fetched primary (author copy)
- Review question: Which motive groups does this system serve? Does it block a player who wants two motives at once, for example achievement and social?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, SOCIAL, FANTASY
- Limits: Self-selected online survey of 2000s MMO players. It shows what players want, not whether a system delivers it.

### "Alone Together": grouping data from 129,372 WoW characters
- Claim: MEASURED: census data on 129,372 WoW characters across five servers, from June 2005. The most soloable classes were the most popular. Characters that never grouped levelled about twice as fast as characters that grouped at any rate. More than half of play time was in groups only from level 59. 66% of characters were in a guild (90% at level 43 and up). Between levels 41 and 60, guilded characters grouped about 43% more often, with play time controlled. The authors say others serve as "an audience, a sense of social presence, and a spectacle".
- Kind: research
- Measured what: retention or play time (grouping and play time), not fun
- Source: "'Alone Together?' Exploring the Social Dynamics of Massively Multiplayer Online Games", Nicolas Ducheneaut, Nick Yee, Eric Nickell, Robert J. Moore, CHI 2006, http://www.nickyee.com/pubs/Ducheneaut,%20Yee,%20Nickell,%20Moore%20-%20Alone%20Together%20(2006).pdf
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: Does grouping cost the player speed in this system? Does the design give value to being seen by others, even without a group?
- Use it for: SOCIAL
- Limits: One game in 2005, observational data. It measured behavior, not enjoyment.

### League of Legends: defaults shape social behavior
- Claim: MEASURED (Riot, 2012 data): Riot turned cross-team chat off by default, with an opt-in. Negative chat dropped 32.7%, neutral chat dropped 1.9%, and positive chat rose 34.5%. Games with cross-team chat fell only from 54% to 53%. MEASURED: about 1% of players are consistently toxic, and they produce only about 5% of toxicity. Most toxicity comes from ordinary players on a bad day.
- Kind: measured
- Measured what: fun or sentiment (chat tone as a proxy)
- Source: "The first online player behavior experiment at Riot Games", Jeffrey Lin, LinkedIn, 2015, https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/first-online-player-behavior-experiment-riot-games-jeffrey-lin . "More carrot, less stick: Jeffrey Lin on tweaking League of Legends player behavior", Christian Nutt, Game Developer, 2015, https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/more-carrot-less-stick-jeffrey-lin-on-tweaking-i-league-of-legends-i-player-behavior
- Verified: fetched primary (Lin's post). The 1% and 5% figures: secondary (Game Developer).
- Review question: What does the default setting of each social channel invite? Does the design assume bad behavior comes from a few bad players?
- Use it for: SOCIAL, UX
- Limits: Competitive PvP among strangers. Co-op with friends has different social risks. Chat tone is a proxy for experience, not fun.

## Player psychology and UX research

### Self-Determination Theory in games (PENS)
- Claim: Four studies found that perceived in-game autonomy and competence go with game enjoyment, game preference, and changes in well-being from before to after play. Intuitive controls and a sense of presence also went with competence and autonomy. In a survey of an online multiplayer community, autonomy, competence, and relatedness each independently predicted enjoyment and future play.
- Kind: research
- Strength: strong (four studies, and a large body of later work builds on it). The data are mostly correlational self-reports.
- Source: (1) "The Motivational Pull of Video Games: A Self-Determination Theory Approach", Richard M. Ryan, C. Scott Rigby, Andrew Przybylski, 2006, Motivation and Emotion 30(4):344-360, https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-006-9051-8 (2) "Glued to Games: How Video Games Draw Us In and Hold Us Spellbound", Scott Rigby and Richard M. Ryan, 2011, Praeger, ISBN 9780313362248 (3) "Self-Determination Theory in HCI Games Research: Current Uses and Open Questions", April Tyack and Elisa D. Mekler, CHI 2020, https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376723
- Verified: fetched primary (full text of paper 1, abstract of paper 3 through OpenAlex). Book 2: secondary (publisher and bookseller listings).
- Review question: For each need (competence, autonomy, relatedness), which mechanic in this system satisfies it, and which mechanic could frustrate it?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, MASTERY, DECISIONS, SOCIAL
- Limits: The studies measure how players rate their experience. They do not tell you how much need satisfaction a specific mechanic gives. Tyack and Mekler reviewed 110 CHI and CHI PLAY papers. They found that few papers engage with SDT "beyond descriptive accounts". So treat "this feature supports autonomy" as a hypothesis to playtest, not as a finding.

### Quantic Foundry Gamer Motivation Model
- Claim: Factor analysis of survey data gives 12 motivations in 6 pairs and 3 clusters. Action-Social: Destruction, Excitement, Competition, Community. Mastery-Achievement: Challenge, Strategy, Completion, Power. Immersion-Creativity: Fantasy, Story, Design, Discovery. Each motivation is a spectrum, not a player type.
- Kind: measured
- Strength: mixed (a very large sample, but it is a self-selected web survey run by a commercial firm)
- Source: "Gamer Motivation Model Reference Sheets and Details (v3)", Quantic Foundry (Nick Yee and Nic Ducheneaut), 2026, https://quanticfoundry.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Gamer-Motivation-Model-Reference-v3.pdf (the 2019 edition is at https://quanticfoundry.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Gamer-Motivation-Model-Reference.pdf)
- Verified: fetched primary. The v3 sheet says "Over 2 million gamers worldwide have participated in our Gamer Motivation Profile" and "Using data from 2M+ gamers, we identified 12 distinct gaming motivations." The 2019 sheet said "Over 1.25M gamers".
- Review question: Which two or three motivations does this system serve? Does it work against a motivation that the target audience scores high on?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, FANTASY, SOCIAL, MASTERY
- Limits: The data come from people who chose to take an online profile quiz. The model describes players, not mechanics. The sheet warns that a low score is also a preference. For example, the low end of Community is "Independence" (single-player, soloable quests, full control).

### Bartle's player types
- Claim: Bartle proposed four MUD player types (Achievers, Explorers, Socialisers, Killers) on two axes: players versus world, and acting versus interacting. He took them from a long debate among experienced MUD administrators ("wizzes"), from November 1989 to May 1990.
- Kind: principle
- Strength: weak (not derived from data, and later factor analysis did not support its core assumption)
- Source: (1) "Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades: Players Who Suit MUDs", Richard A. Bartle, 1996, https://mud.co.uk/richard/hcds.htm (2) "Motivations for Play in Online Games", Nick Yee, 2006, CyberPsychology and Behavior 9(6), https://doi.org/10.1089/cpb.2006.9.772 (preprint: https://nickyee.com/pubs/Yee%20-%20Motivations%20(2007).pdf)
- Verified: fetched primary (Bartle essay and Yee preprint)
- Review question: If the doc names player types, does it treat them as overlapping motives that one player can have at the same time? Or does it treat them as separate audiences?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, SOCIAL
- Limits: Bartle writes: "I am not a trained psychologist, do not expect a conventionally rigorous approach". Yee collected data from 3000 MMORPG players. He found that play motivations "do not suppress each other as Bartle suggested" (correlations among the 3 main components r < .10). Bartle built the model for MUDs. Use it as shared vocabulary only.

### Hodent: The Gamer's Brain (usability and engage-ability)
- Claim: Game UX needs usability ("ease of use") and "engage-ability" ("the ability of the game to be fun and engaging"). Hodent's seven usability pillars are: signs and feedback, clarity, form follows function, consistency, minimum workload, error prevention and recovery, flexibility and accessibility. Engage-ability rests on motivation, emotion, and game flow. At Epic, playtests showed that players read an early Fortnite trap icon as ammunition or trees. The team redrew it as a bear trap.
- Kind: principle
- Strength: mixed (built on cognitive science, but the framework is a practitioner synthesis, not a tested model)
- Source: (1) "The Gamer's Brain: How Neuroscience and UX Can Impact Video Game Design", Celia Hodent, 2017, CRC Press, ISBN 9781498775502, https://thegamersbrain.com/ (2) "Understanding the success of Fortnite: UX", Celia Hodent, https://celiahodent.com/understanding-the-success-of-fortnite-ux/ (3) "The Gamer's Brain, part 3" (GDC 2017), https://celiahodent.com/gamers-brain-part-3-ux-engagement-immersion-retention-gdc17-talk/
- Verified: fetched primary (the author's book site and blog). I did not read the book text. The pillar names come from the author's blog.
- Review question: For each new element in the system, what sign tells the player it exists? What feedback confirms each action? How much physical and mental workload does it add?
- Use it for: UX, MASTERY, MOTIVATION
- Limits: The pillars find friction. They do not rank fun. Some difficulty is intended, so do not flag an intended challenge as a usability defect.

### Games user research: Halo 3 and Valve playtesting
- Claim: Microsoft's lab, led by Randy Pagulayan, "analyzed more than 3,000 hours of Halo 3 played by some 600 everyday gamers". The lab tracked "how and where ... players most frequently get killed". Valve's slides say "Game designs are hypotheses" and "Playtests are experiments". They stress the "Importance of what people do, not what they say". The slides also show a death heatmap for the TF2 map Dustbowl.
- Kind: postmortem
- Strength: strong as industry practice (two large studios describe the same method). The sources describe the method but publish few before-and-after results.
- Source: (1) "Halo 3: How Microsoft Labs Invented a New Science of Play", Clive Thompson, Wired, August 2007, reprint at https://abcnews.com/Technology/story?id=3506473 (2) "Valve's Approach to Playtesting: The Application of Empiricism", Mike Ambinder, GDC 2009, https://cdn.akamai.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2009/GDC2009_ValvesApproachToPlaytesting.pdf
- Verified: fetched primary for the Valve slides. Wired blocked the fetch, so I read the ABC News reprint of the same article.
- Review question: What does the doc predict players will do in this system? Which playtest observation or telemetry (for example, a death or quit heatmap) will confirm or reject each prediction?
- Use it for: UX, MASTERY
- Limits: The Wired article names problems but does not document the fixes. Examples: grenades "aren't visible enough", and an enemy "can kill the player in three shots". Valve's slides warn that "Averages hide extreme examples" and that stats "Can see 'illusory' patterns". Telemetry shows where, not why.

### Heuristic evaluation for games (HEP, PLAY, Pinelle, Nielsen)
- Claim: HEP lists 43 playability heuristics in four groups: game play (16), game story (8), mechanics (7), and usability (12). On one early prototype, HEP found more issues than a study with four users. The user study found issues more specific to the game. Pinelle et al. analyzed reviews of 108 PC games (18 from each of 6 genres). They found twelve classes of usability problems and wrote ten game usability heuristics. Nielsen calls his 10 heuristics "broad rules of thumb and not specific usability guidelines".
- Kind: research
- Strength: mixed (useful early in design, but validation is small: the authors checked HEP on one prototype with four users)
- Source: (1) "Using heuristics to evaluate the playability of games", Heather Desurvire, Martin Caplan, Jozsef A. Toth, CHI 2004 Extended Abstracts, https://doi.org/10.1145/985921.986102 (2) "Game Usability Heuristics (PLAY) for Evaluating and Designing Better Games: The Next Iteration", Heather Desurvire and Charlotte Wiberg, 2009, LNCS (HCII 2009), https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-02774-1_60 (3) "Heuristic evaluation for games: usability principles for video game design", David Pinelle, Nelson Wong, Tadeusz Stach, CHI 2008, https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357282 (4) "10 Usability Heuristics for User Interface Design", Jakob Nielsen, 1994 (page updated 2024), https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/
- Verified: fetched primary (HEP full text, Pinelle abstract through OpenAlex, NN/g page). PLAY: secondary (Crossref and Springer listing only).
- Review question: Is each goal of the system visible? Is the result of each action clear? Can the player recover from errors? Is "the first player action ... painfully obvious" with "immediate positive feedback" (HEP game play heuristic 13)?
- Use it for: UX
- Limits: The HEP authors say it is "best suited for evaluating general issues in the early development phases". Heuristics predict problems. They do not replace playtests. Nielsen made his list for productivity software, where the goal is ease. Games want some difficulty.

### Game accessibility guidelines (GAG and XAG)
- Claim: The Game Accessibility Guidelines sort advice into basic, intermediate, and advanced tiers. Basic items include "Allow controls to be remapped / reconfigured", "Ensure no essential information is conveyed by a fixed colour alone", "Ensure no essential information is conveyed by sounds alone", and "Offer a wide choice of difficulty levels". The Xbox Accessibility Guidelines (version 3.2, June 8, 2023) give a goal, scoping questions, and implementation guidance for each topic.
- Kind: principle
- Strength: strong as practice (industry consensus with input from disabled players). It is not experimental evidence.
- Source: (1) Game Accessibility Guidelines, "a collaborative effort between a group of studios, specialists and academics", since 2012, https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/ (basic list: https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/basic/) (2) Xbox Accessibility Guidelines, Microsoft, v3.2, 2023, https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/guidelines
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: Does this system carry essential information only through color, only through sound, or only through fast timing? Can the player remap its inputs?
- Use it for: UX
- Limits: Microsoft says the XAGs "aren't intended to act as a checklist to validate any type of compliance or legal requirements". The guidelines do not choose between options that change the design intent (for example, difficulty choices). The team must decide those.

### Loss aversion and framing
- Claim: Prospect theory says the value function "is generally steeper for losses than for gains". A 2024 meta-analysis of 607 estimates from 150 articles found a mean loss aversion coefficient of 1.955. A 2025 re-analysis of the same data looked at studies where gains and losses were the same size and not ordered by size. In those studies, the coefficient was about 1.07, not significantly above 1.0. The core decision-under-risk patterns of prospect theory replicated in 19 countries with 4,098 participants.
- Kind: research
- Strength: mixed (framing and risk patterns replicate well, but the size of loss aversion depends on how the choice is built)
- Source: (1) "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk", Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, 1979, Econometrica 47(2), https://doi.org/10.2307/1914185 (2) "Meta-analysis of Empirical Estimates of Loss Aversion", Brown, Imai, Vieider, Camerer, 2024, Journal of Economic Literature 62(2):485-516, https://doi.org/10.1257/jel.20221698 (3) "Loss aversion is not robust: A re-meta-analysis", Eldad Yechiam and Dana Zeif, 2025, Journal of Economic Psychology 107:102801, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joep.2025.102801 (4) "Replicating patterns of prospect theory for decision under risk", Ruggeri et al., 2020, Nature Human Behaviour 4:622-633, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-020-0886-x (5) "The Loss of Loss Aversion: Will It Loom Larger Than Its Gain?", David Gal and Derek Rucker, 2018, Journal of Consumer Psychology 28(3):497-516, https://doi.org/10.1002/jcpy.1047
- Verified: fetched primary for 1 (abstract), 2 (AEA abstract), and 3 (author preprint). Secondary for 4 (Crossref metadata and a phys.org report) and 5 (Crossref metadata and search summaries).
- Review question: Where does the system take something from the player (a death penalty, decay, durability)? Could the same numbers be framed as a bonus for the wanted behavior? Has a playtest compared the two framings?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, DECISIONS
- Limits: Do not assume "losses hurt twice as much" as a fixed rule. The well-known World of Warcraft rest-system story is a reported anecdote with no survey data. See "WoW rest system: a bonus, not a penalty".

### Overjustification effect
- Claim: In Lepper, Greene, and Nisbett (1973), some children agreed to do an activity for an expected award. Later, they showed less free-choice interest in it than children who got an unexpected award or no award. A meta-analysis of 128 studies found that engagement-, completion-, and performance-contingent rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.40, -0.36, -0.28). Positive feedback enhanced free-choice behavior (d = 0.33) and self-reported interest (d = 0.31).
- Kind: research
- Strength: mixed (the effect of expected tangible rewards is solid in lab tasks, but a rival review says the harm occurs "under highly restricted, easily avoidable conditions")
- Source: (1) "Effects of externally mediated rewards on intrinsic motivation", Edward L. Deci, 1971, JPSP 18(1):105-115, https://doi.org/10.1037/h0030644 (2) "Undermining children's intrinsic interest with extrinsic reward: A test of the 'overjustification' hypothesis", Lepper, Greene, Nisbett, 1973, JPSP 28(1):129-137, https://doi.org/10.1037/h0035519 (3) "A Meta-Analytic Review of Experiments Examining the Effects of Extrinsic Rewards on Intrinsic Motivation", Deci, Koestner, Ryan, 1999, Psychological Bulletin 125(6):627-668, https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627 (4) "Detrimental effects of reward: Reality or myth?", Eisenberger and Cameron, 1996, American Psychologist 51(11):1153-1166, https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.51.11.1153
- Verified: fetched primary for 2 and 3 (full text). Secondary for 1 (Crossref metadata) and 4 (PubMed abstract through search).
- Review question: Does the system pay an expected, tangible reward for an activity that players already enjoy? Could feedback that shows skill replace or reduce it?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, MASTERY
- Limits: Most studies use short lab tasks with children or students. The 1999 meta-analysis found tangible rewards "tended to be more detrimental for children than college students". In games, many rewards are part of play itself, so the lab setup does not map one to one.

### Game elements as extrinsic incentives (points, levels, leaderboards)
- Claim: The study was a 2 x 4 online experiment with an image annotation task. Points, levels, and leaderboards did not significantly change competence or intrinsic motivation compared with a control. Points, and especially levels and the leaderboard, significantly raised performance. The authors conclude that the elements acted as extrinsic incentives.
- Kind: research
- Strength: mixed (one controlled experiment on a non-game task)
- Source: "Towards understanding the effects of individual gamification elements on intrinsic motivation and performance", Elisa D. Mekler, Florian Brühlmann, Alexandre N. Tuch, Klaus Opwis, 2017, Computers in Human Behavior 71:525-534, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2015.08.048
- Verified: secondary (abstract on the Aalto University research portal, search summaries, Crossref metadata)
- Review question: If the system adds points, levels, or ranks on top of an activity, which exact behavior do they push? Is that the behavior the design wants?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, MASTERY
- Limits: One short study with a work-like task. It shows no gain in intrinsic motivation, and also no loss. It says nothing about long-term play.

### Ownership effects: IKEA effect and endowment effect
- Claim: People value things they built. In four studies (IKEA boxes, origami, Legos), labor raised valuation "only when labor results in successful completion of tasks". When participants built and then destroyed their creations, or failed to finish them, the effect dissipated. A meta-analysis (k = 55, N = 5,454) found a moderate effect (d = 0.57). The endowment effect is contested. The claim is that owners ask more to sell a thing than buyers will pay for it. Plott and Zeiler found no gap for mugs after they trained subjects on the valuation procedure. Isoni, Loomes, and Sugden found a persistent gap for lotteries under the same procedure.
- Kind: research
- Strength: mixed (the IKEA effect holds up in a meta-analysis, and the endowment effect depends on procedure)
- Source: (1) "The IKEA effect: When labor leads to love", Michael I. Norton, Daniel Mochon, Dan Ariely, 2012, Journal of Consumer Psychology 22(3):453-460, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.08.002 (2) "Labor Leads to Love, Right? A Meta-Analysis of the IKEA Effect", Pelled, Demetriades, Walter, Psychology and Marketing 43:388-400 (online 2025), https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.70064 (3) "Experimental Tests of the Endowment Effect and the Coase Theorem", Kahneman, Knetsch, Thaler, 1990, Journal of Political Economy 98(6):1325-1348, https://doi.org/10.1086/261737 (4) Plott and Zeiler, 2005, American Economic Review 95(3):530-545, https://doi.org/10.1257/0002828054201387 (5) Isoni, Loomes, Sugden, 2011, American Economic Review 101(2):991-1011, https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.101.2.991
- Verified: fetched primary abstracts for 1, 2, 4, and 5 (Crossref and OpenAlex). Secondary for 3 (Crossref metadata only).
- Review question: Does the player build something in this system (a build, a base, a gear set)? Can they finish it? Does the design later destroy or reset it?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, FANTASY, DECISIONS
- Limits: Destroyed or unfinished creations did not gain value in Norton's studies. The studies use physical products and money valuations, not game items.

### Choice overload
- Claim: In the jam study, 60% (145 of 242) of passing shoppers stopped at a 24-jam display. At a 6-jam display, 40% (104 of 260) stopped. Of those who stopped, nearly 30% (31) bought jam in the 6-jam condition and 3% (4) in the 24-jam condition. A meta-analysis of 63 conditions from 50 experiments (N = 5,036) found "a mean effect size of virtually zero but considerable variance between studies". A later meta-analysis (99 observations, N = 7,202) found four conditions that cause overload: complex choice sets, difficult tasks, uncertain preferences, and a goal to minimize effort.
- Kind: research
- Strength: mixed (a famous single result, a near-zero mean effect, and clear moderators)
- Source: (1) "When choice is demotivating: Can one desire too much of a good thing?", Sheena S. Iyengar and Mark R. Lepper, 2000, JPSP 79(6):995-1006, https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995 (2) "Can There Ever Be Too Many Options? A Meta-Analytic Review of Choice Overload", Scheibehenne, Greifeneder, Todd, 2010, Journal of Consumer Research 37(3):409-425, https://doi.org/10.1086/651235 (3) "Choice overload: A conceptual review and meta-analysis", Chernev, Böckenholt, Goodman, 2015, Journal of Consumer Psychology 25(2):333-358, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002
- Verified: fetched primary (jam study full text, both meta-analysis abstracts)
- Review question: Where the system shows many options (talents, items, recipes), are they complex and hard to compare? Does the player know yet what they want?
- Use it for: DECISIONS, UX
- Limits: Chernev et al. found a significant overall effect once they included the moderators. They say this finding is "counter to the data reported by prior meta-analytic research". Large sets can help experts with clear preferences. This is not a rule to cut options.

### Peak-end rule
- Claim: In a short trial, subjects held a hand in 14 °C water for 60 s. In a long trial, they held it for the same 60 s and then 30 s more while the water warmed slightly. A significant majority chose to repeat the long trial. A meta-analysis of 174 effect sizes found a large peak-end effect on retrospective evaluations (r = 0.581). It found that the effect of duration "was essentially nil".
- Kind: research
- Strength: strong (large meta-analytic effect)
- Source: (1) "When More Pain Is Preferred to Less: Adding a Better End", Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber, Redelmeier, 1993, Psychological Science 4(6):401-405, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x (2) "All's well that ends (and peaks) well? A meta-analysis of the peak-end rule and duration neglect", Alaybek et al., 2022, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 170:104149, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2022.104149
- Verified: fetched primary for 1 (abstract through Crossref). Secondary for 2 (full abstract on EconPapers).
- Review question: What is the most intense moment of a session with this system? Does a session end on a high point or on a chore?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, UX
- Limits: The meta-analysis found that the effect of the overall average was "comparable" to the peak-end effect. So a bad middle still counts. The rule is about how people remember an episode. It does not measure moment-to-moment fun or whether players come back.

### Goal-gradient and endowed progress
- Claim: Café customers bought coffee more often as they got closer to a free coffee. Some customers got a 12-stamp card with 2 "bonus" stamps already on it. They completed the 10 needed purchases faster than customers with a plain 10-stamp card. In a separate study, an 8-step task became a 10-step task with 2 steps already done. This "increases the likelihood of task completion and decreases completion time".
- Kind: research
- Strength: strong (field experiments with real customers, and the pattern repeats)
- Source: (1) "The Goal-Gradient Hypothesis Resurrected: Purchase Acceleration, Illusionary Goal Progress, and Customer Retention", Ran Kivetz, Oleg Urminsky, Yuhuang Zheng, 2006, Journal of Marketing Research 43(1):39-58, https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39 (2) "The Endowed Progress Effect: How Artificial Advancement Increases Effort", Joseph C. Nunes and Xavier Drèze, 2006, Journal of Consumer Research 32(4), https://doi.org/10.1086/500480
- Verified: fetched primary (abstracts through Crossref and OpenAlex)
- Review question: How does the system show the distance to the next goal? Does progress start at zero or with a visible head start?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, UX
- Limits: The evidence comes from loyalty programs and a song-rating site, not games. Nunes and Drèze list two moderators: the reason given for the head start, and the "currency in which progress is recorded".

### Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects
- Claim: A 2025 meta-analysis "found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks but found a general tendency to resume tasks". The authors conclude "the Ovsiankina effect represents a general tendency, whereas the Zeigarnik effect lacks universal validity".
- Kind: research
- Strength: mixed (the resumption tendency holds, and the memory claim does not)
- Source: "Interruption, recall and resumption: a meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects", Ghibellini and Meier, 2025, Humanities and Social Sciences Communications 12:962, https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w
- Verified: fetched primary (abstract through Crossref)
- Review question: Does the design depend on unfinished things (open quests, cliffhangers, partial progress)? If yes, does it make them easy to resume, instead of betting that players will remember them?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, UX
- Limits: The resumption data come from lab tasks. They do not show that a player will return to a game after days away.

### Variable-ratio rewards and loot boxes
- Claim: Hopson wrote that variable ratio schedules give "the highest overall rates of activity of all the schedules". A survey of 7,422 gamers linked loot box spending with problem gambling severity (eta squared = 0.054). A replication used 1,172 gamers who did not self-select into a loot box study. It found a similar link (eta squared = 0.051). In a systematic review, 12 of 13 publications reported a positive link between loot boxes and problem gambling (mean r = .27).
- Kind: research
- Strength: strong for the correlation. The direction of cause is unknown.
- Source: (1) "Behavioral Game Design", John Hopson, Gamasutra, 2001, https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/behavioral-game-design (2) "Video game loot boxes are linked to problem gambling: Results of a large-scale survey", David Zendle and Paul Cairns, 2018, PLOS ONE 13(11):e0206767, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0206767 (3) "Loot boxes are again linked to problem gambling: Results of a replication study", Zendle and Cairns, 2019, PLOS ONE 14(3):e0213194, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0213194 (4) "Loot boxes, problem gambling and problem video gaming: A systematic review and meta-synthesis", Spicer et al., 2022, New Media and Society 24(4):1001-1022, https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448211027175
- Verified: fetched primary (Hopson article, PMC full text of 2, abstracts of 3 and 4)
- Review question: Does the system give random rewards? Can players pay real money for the random draws? What protects the players who spend the most?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION
- Limits: Zendle and Cairns say it is "impossible to tease apart" the direction. Loot boxes could lead to problem gambling, or problem gamblers could spend more on loot boxes. The studies cover paid loot boxes, not random drops that players earn only through play. Hopson notes: "This is not to say that players are the same as rats".

### Working memory limits (Miller and Cowan)
- Claim: Miller (1956) summarized evidence that people can hold about seven chunks. Cowan (2001) says that number "was meant more as a rough estimate and a rhetorical device than as a real capacity limit". He argues for "a single, central capacity limit averaging about four chunks" when rehearsal and chunking are blocked.
- Kind: research
- Strength: strong (a broad review of many data sets)
- Source: (1) "The magical number seven, plus or minus two: Some limits on our capacity for processing information", George A. Miller, 1956, Psychological Review 63(2):81-97, https://doi.org/10.1037/h0043158 (2) "The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of mental storage capacity", Nelson Cowan, 2001, Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24(1):87-114, https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922
- Verified: fetched primary for 2 (abstract through Crossref). Secondary for 1 (Crossref metadata and Cowan's summary).
- Review question: How many new items must a player hold in mind, with nothing on screen, to use this system correctly?
- Use it for: UX, DECISIONS
- Limits: The limit is for recall without cues. Menus and visible options use recognition. Experts group more items into each chunk, so they can hold more.

### Game feel and juice
- Claim: Swink defines game feel as "Real-time control of virtual objects in a simulated space, with interactions emphasized by polish." Jonasson and Purho (2012) and Nijman (2013) showed live how added feedback (particles, screen shake, sound) changes the feel of a simple game. Kao studied 3,018 players of one action RPG. Both "None and Extreme amounts of juiciness" lowered play time, player experience, intrinsic motivation, and performance compared with Medium and High. A separate study found that visual embellishments made all tested games more visually appealing. They affected aspects such as competence only "under specific circumstances".
- Kind: principle (with research checks)
- Strength: mixed (strong craft consensus, but the research shows an inverted-U, not "more is better")
- Source: (1) "Game Feel: A Game Designer's Guide to Virtual Sensation", Steve Swink, 2008, Morgan Kaufmann, ISBN 9780123743282 (2) "Juice It or Lose It", Martin Jonasson and Petri Purho, GDC Europe 2012, https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1016487/juice-it-or-lose (3) "The Art of Screenshake", Jan Willem Nijman, INDIGO Classes 2013, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AJdEqssNZ-U (4) "The effects of juiciness in an action RPG", Dominic Kao, 2020, Entertainment Computing 34:100359, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.entcom.2020.100359 (5) "Juicy Game Design: Understanding the Impact of Visual Embellishments on Player Experience", Hicks, Gerling, Dickinson, Vanden Abeele, CHI PLAY 2019, https://doi.org/10.1145/3311350.3347171
- Verified: fetched primary for 1 (chapter 1 definition), 4, and 5 (abstracts). Secondary for 2 and 3 (GDC Vault and YouTube listings). I did not watch the talks.
- Review question: For each player action in this system, what visual, audio, and haptic feedback confirms it? Does any of that feedback hide game state?
- Use it for: MASTERY, UX, FANTASY
- Limits: Kao tested one game, so the best level of juice depends on the game. Swink's definition centers on real-time control. It fits menus and turn-based systems poorly.

### Tutorials and game complexity
- Claim: A study of over 45,000 players tested eight tutorial designs in three games. Tutorials raised play time by as much as 29% in the most complex game (Foldit). They "did not significantly improve player engagement in the two simpler games". Instructions given at the moment of need raised play time by 16% and progress by 40%, in Foldit only. The authors "found no evidence" that restricting player freedom improves learnability. On-demand help had negative effects in Refraction.
- Kind: research
- Strength: strong (a large field experiment with many tutorial variants)
- Source: "The impact of tutorials on games of varying complexity", Erik Andersen, Eleanor O'Rourke, Yun-En Liu, Richard Snider, Jeff Lowdermilk, David Truong, Seth Cooper, Zoran Popović, CHI 2012, https://doi.org/10.1145/2207676.2207687 (PDF: http://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/chi2012.pdf)
- Verified: fetched primary (full text)
- Review question: Can players learn this system's rules by experimenting? If not, where will the game teach each rule at the moment it is needed?
- Use it for: UX, MASTERY
- Limits: The games were casual browser games. The study measured play time and progress, not enjoyment. The authors say tutorial investment "may not be justified for games with mechanics that can be discovered through experimentation". That is a conditional claim.

### Teaching through level design (Portal) and learning by doing
- Claim: Valve's Portal commentary describes chamber changes made after playtests. "Early versions of Portal let players stumble through the beginning of the game without always understanding what was going on" (Robin Walker). "Portal momentum ended up being the hardest concept to convey", so Valve introduced it "slowly, step by step" (Garret Rickey). For mid-air portal placement, Valve built one chamber "so that the only way to solve it, was to use the mid-air placement tactic". Also: "without some serious prompting, players will rarely look up" (Jeep Barnett). Hodent writes: "When you teach the player something when they can actually do it, it gives context (learning by doing)."
- Kind: postmortem
- Strength: mixed (first-hand developer accounts, but no data published)
- Source: (1) Portal developer commentary, Valve, 2007, transcript at https://theportalwiki.com/wiki/Portal_developer_commentary (2) "The Gamer's Brain, part 2: The UX of onboarding and player engagement", Celia Hodent, https://celiahodent.com/gamers-brain-ux-onboarding/
- Verified: secondary for 1 (fan wiki transcript of the in-game commentary). Fetched primary for 2.
- Review question: For each rule of this system, which encounter makes the player use it once in a safe space before it matters?
- Use it for: UX, MASTERY
- Limits: Portal is linear. An open or systemic design cannot always put a lesson behind one required puzzle.

### Cognitive challenge and immersion
- Claim: More physical demand (more interaction with the game) did not raise immersion. Time pressure, which made the game more physically and cognitively challenging, did raise immersion. The authors argue that the challenge a player feels is "an interaction between the level of expertise of the gamer and the cognitive challenge encompassed within the game".
- Kind: research
- Strength: mixed (three lab studies)
- Source: "Not doing but thinking: the role of challenge in the gaming experience", Anna Cox, Paul Cairns, Pari Shah, Michael Carroll, CHI 2012, pp. 79-88, https://doi.org/10.1145/2207676.2207689
- Verified: fetched primary (abstract through OpenAlex)
- Review question: Is the challenge in this system about thinking (planning, reading the situation)? Or is it only about doing more inputs, faster?
- Use it for: MASTERY, DECISIONS
- Limits: Short lab sessions with questionnaire measures of immersion. Time pressure changed both physical and cognitive demand, so the study does not fully separate the two.

### Positive failure (avatar death and high challenge)
- Claim: A survey of 95 players right after the release of Dark Souls III found that players "generally enjoyed challenging play sessions and mostly reported positive experiences". Achievement and learning moments drove positive experiences. Those moments "were enabled by negative events such as difficulties and avatar death".
- Kind: research
- Strength: mixed (one survey with a self-selected sample)
- Source: "A Good Reason to Die: How Avatar Death and High Challenges Enable Positive Experiences", Serge Petralito, Florian Brühlmann, Glena Iten, Elisa D. Mekler, Klaus Opwis, CHI 2017, pp. 5087-5097, https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3026047 (PDF: http://www.mmi-basel.ch/extras/2017_Petralito.pdf)
- Verified: fetched primary (full text)
- Review question: When the player fails in this system, what do they learn? Does the next attempt let them use it?
- Use it for: MASTERY, MOTIVATION
- Limits: The players chose a game famous for difficulty. The results may not hold for players who avoid such games.

### Flow and GameFlow
- Claim: GameFlow maps flow onto eight elements: concentration, challenge, skills, control, clear goals, feedback, immersion, and social interaction. Experts reviewed two real-time strategy games with the criteria. The criteria told the high-rated game apart from the low-rated one. Chen argues that "a well-designed game transports its players to their personal Flow Zones".
- Kind: principle
- Strength: mixed (the only check of GameFlow was expert reviews of two RTS games, and Chen's piece is an essay)
- Source: (1) "GameFlow: a model for evaluating player enjoyment in games", Penelope Sweetser and Peta Wyeth, 2005, Computers in Entertainment 3(3), https://doi.org/10.1145/1077246.1077253 (2) "Flow in games (and everything else)", Jenova Chen, 2007, Communications of the ACM 50(4):31-34, https://doi.org/10.1145/1232743.1232769
- Verified: fetched primary (abstracts through OpenAlex)
- Review question: Does the system give clear goals and immediate feedback? Does its challenge grow as the player's skill grows?
- Use it for: MASTERY, UX
- Limits: Flow is one kind of fun. Petralito et al. show positive experiences built on repeated failure. A strict "challenge must match skill" reading would flag that as a defect.

### Dynamic difficulty adjustment (DDA)
- Claim: Hunicke notes the conventional wisdom that players "will feel 'cheated' if games are adjusted". She presents a DDA system (Hamlet). Her preliminary results "challenge common assumptions about player enjoyment and adjustment dynamics". Constant and Levieux tested DDA in three games (logical, motor, sensory). They found that DDA "can lead players to high level of overconfidence", measured with an in-game bet.
- Kind: research
- Strength: weak (preliminary results and small lab studies)
- Source: (1) "The case for dynamic difficulty adjustment in games", Robin Hunicke, ACE 2005, pp. 429-433, https://doi.org/10.1145/1178477.1178573 (2) "Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment Impact on Players' Confidence", Thomas Constant and Guillaume Levieux, CHI 2019, https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300693
- Verified: fetched primary (abstracts through OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar)
- Review question: If the system adjusts difficulty, will players notice it? Will it weaken their sense that a success was earned?
- Use it for: MASTERY, MOTIVATION
- Limits: Constant and Levieux suggest the confidence boost "may be a part of the positive impact of DDA systems", so overconfidence is not always harm. Neither paper tests long-term trust after players find out about the adjustment.

## System precedents: Loot

### Path of Exile Trade Manifesto

He wrote: "We believe that it is more fun to slowly and iteratively upgrade a character over time and to have a longer journey to gear a character up." He also argued that easy trade forces lower drop rates. He said trade had already made the game "very hard for some players and quite easy for others." GGG kept friction in trade on purpose.

## System precedents: Builds

### Diablo III: skill points removed for limited skill slots

- Use it for: DECISIONS, MASTERY
- Limits: This is a pre-launch statement of intent, not a measured result. Free swaps move build identity onto slot choice and items. The doc must then give items and slots that job.

### Grim Dawn Devotion constellations

## System precedents: Skills and grinding

### "Water Finds a Crack" (Morrowind skill grinding)
- Claim: Soren Johnson wrote: "Given the opportunity, players will optimize the fun out of a game." His example: Morrowind's skill system "rewards players for repeating any activity. Running into a wall for hours increases the Athletics skill while jumping over and over again increases the Acrobatics skill." He wrote that players "will trade time for safety" and can undervalue their own time. His remedies put the work on the designer: change rules so a dominant strategy stops dominating, and give options. He concluded: "the designer can't go wrong putting the player in control of his or her own experience."
- Kind: principle
- Source: GD Column 17: Water Finds a Crack, Soren Johnson (Game Developer magazine, March 2011 issue), 2011, https://www.designer-notes.com/game-developer-column-17-water-finds-a-crack/
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: For each use-based skill, what is the lowest-risk way to raise it? Is that way also fun? If a player did it for an hour, would they enjoy that hour?
- Use it for: MASTERY, MOTIVATION, DECISIONS
- Limits: A design essay with examples, not data. It applies to any "do X to get better at X" system, including crafting and gathering skills.

### Skyrim removes attributes but keeps "do it to get better"
- Claim: Former Skyrim design lead Bruce Nesmith said he "led the charge" to remove Oblivion-style attributes. He said the goal was "getting the character system out of the player's way." He said: "We got rid of attributes in Skyrim and you know who complained? Almost nobody." He added: "I love the whole idea that you do something, you get better at it."
- Kind: postmortem
- Source: "We got rid of attributes in Skyrim and you know who complained? Almost nobody", Austin Wood (GamesRadar+, reporting a Press Box PR interview), 2026, https://www.gamesradar.com/games/rpg/we-got-rid-of-attributes-in-skyrim-and-you-know-who-complained-almost-nobody-former-bethesda-lead-pushed-for-a-streamlined-rpg-where-you-dont-have-your-head-buried-in-menus-stats-and-rules/
- Verified: secondary (GamesRadar+)

- Use it for: MASTERY, UX

### Old School RuneScape: a new skill must earn its place
- Claim: In 2019 the proposed Warding skill got 82,633 yes votes (66.4%) of 125,147. The pass threshold was 75%, so "Warding will not be added to the game." In October 2022 Jagex lowered the threshold to 70%. Jagex said "Fewer than 6% of active members actually vote in polls," and at 75% "it only takes 1.5% of those active members voting no to stop a piece of content." In 2023 Sailing passed. Jagex said the vote "was generally consistent, starting at 76% and remaining around 71-2%" with 161,381 voters. - Kind: measured

- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: If players who love the current skills saw this new skill, what would they say it adds that no current skill gives? What does it take away from them?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, MASTERY, SOCIAL
- Limits: These votes measure stated preference in a live game with a devoted, conservative player base. They are not play data. A new game has no existing skill set to protect.

## System precedents: Town and base

### RimWorld: a story generator with a chosen storyteller
- Claim: Ludeon describes RimWorld as "a story generator" that is "designed to co-author tragic, twisted, and triumphant stories." An AI storyteller controls random events. Players choose the storyteller before play: Randy Random "does crazy stuff," Cassandra Classic "goes for rising tension," and Phoebe Chillax "likes to relax."
- Kind: principle
- Source: RimWorld official site, Ludeon Studios, undated, https://rimworldgame.com/
- Verified: fetched primary
- Review question: Who controls the pacing of threats to the town? Can the player choose the style of pressure before play, not only the amount?
- Use it for: FANTASY, DECISIONS, MOTIVATION
- Limits: A colony simulation where the player accepts loss as story. In an action RPG, the town is often the player's safe place, so the same pressure may feel worse.

### Against the Storm: send the player away before stability turns to boredom
- Claim: Designer Michał Ogłoziński said: "Stability is the ultimate goal of city builders and once the player achieves it, we as designers usually have two options: we can either poke the player out of this balanced state or let them get bored." - Kind: principle
- Source: "How Against the Storm managed to mix city-building and roguelite play", Joel Couture (Game Developer), 2023, https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-against-the-storm-managed-to-mix-city-building-and-roguelite-play
- Verified: fetched primary (developer Q&A in Game Developer)
- Review question: What happens after the town is "done"? Does the design poke the player, end the run, or let them get bored?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, DECISIONS
- Limits: Against the Storm resets each town. A persistent hub town cannot use the "send them away" answer. It needs a different poke.

### Fallout 4 radiant settlement quests
- Claim: Preston Garvey hands out repeating settlement quests. Player reaction (reported by Kotaku, not a developer): "Whenever you finish one of these quests, Preston always has another one waiting for you." The quests became an internet joke. - Limits: This is player sentiment and a meme, not measured data. It shows a risk of generated upkeep quests. It does not show that all repeatable town tasks fail.

## System precedents: Failure and meta-progression

### Diablo III difficulty rework
- Claim: Mosqueira said the launch game "was so hard, that instead of being these epic heroes, fighting against the forces of darkness, you were a Barbarian smashing pots." Patch 2.0.1 removed Normal, Nightmare, Hell and Inferno. It replaced them with "dynamic difficulty," with monsters that level with the character. Five settings became selectable "at-will," and "Each level offers new bonuses and rewards." Players could lower the difficulty in a game.
- Kind: postmortem
- Source: 2.0.1 PTR and Closed Beta Patch Notes, Nevalistis (Blizzard Entertainment), 2013, http://web.archive.org/web/20140101173655/http://us.battle.net/d3/en/blog/11883960/ and the PCGamesN GDC 2015 report (Loot section)
- Verified: fetched primary (Wayback copy) and secondary (PCGamesN)
- Review question: When the game is too hard, what is the best-paying activity? Is it the fantasy activity, or something degenerate?
- Use it for: FANTASY, MOTIVATION, MASTERY
- Limits: An online ARPG with repeated content. Selectable difficulty with scaled rewards fits loot games. It may fit less well in a game where one fixed challenge is the point.

### Hades God Mode
- Claim: Greg Kasavin said: "Our big focus from the start with Hades was how to take the sting of failure and reduce that as much as possible." He said roguelikes are interesting because they are "different every time you play. To experience that, they've got to kill you." Supergiant did not weaken enemies. They asked: "What if we just make you a little bit tougher?" God Mode starts at 20 percent less damage taken and adds two percent after each death, up to 80 percent. Kasavin said difficulty settings "may need to be proprietary to the game. It's not a one size fits all solution."
- Kind: principle
- Source: "Hades devs reveal how God Mode solves the worst thing about the genre", Tomas Franzese (Inverse), 2021, https://www.inverse.com/gaming/hades-god-mode-interview and "Hades God Mode explained by Supergiant Games", Ben Bayliss (Can I Play That?), 2021, https://caniplaythat.com/2021/08/11/hades-god-mode-explained-by-supergiant-games/
- Verified: fetched primary (developer interview in Inverse) and secondary (Can I Play That?, quoting Kasavin)
- Review question: If a player fails many times, what grows: their options, their power, or only their frustration? Is help opt-in and shaped around this game's core loop?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, MASTERY, UX
- Limits: Hades has short runs, so death is frequent. Kasavin himself doubted the design transfers to other games as-is. No usage data was published in these sources.

### Celeste Assist Mode and its framing
- Claim: Assist Mode lets players change the game's rules. The first text called difficulty "essential." After feedback from accessibility advocates, the team changed it to "intended." Designer Matt Thorson said the old text "was unintentionally undermining" the goal "to include even more people." At launch the game also "branded" save files that used Assist Mode with an icon. The team removed it quickly, because "the reasoning that got us to that design feels like the same exclusionary reasoning that affected the preamble text."
- Kind: postmortem
- Source: "The Small But Important Change 'Celeste' Made to Its Celebrated Assist Mode", Patrick Klepek (Vice), 2019, https://www.vice.com/en/article/celeste-assist-mode-change-and-accessibility/
- Verified: secondary (Vice, with direct quotes from Thorson)
- Review question: How does the doc word its help options? Does it mark or shame the players who use them?
- Use it for: UX, MOTIVATION
- Limits: This is about framing and inclusion, not a play-data result. It applies to any opt-in help system.

### Dead Cells: death as progression
- Claim: At GDC 2019, Sebastien Benard of Motion Twin said: "Dying should be like 'woo yeah, I died, so I can do something new!'" The team made "death as progression" central, so death opens new areas. For platforming, they asked, "Is the player meant to die doing this?" If the answer is no, "we just make sure the player succeeds."
- Kind: principle
- Source: "The secrets of Dead Cells' smart constraints", Danielle Riendeau (Game Developer), 2025, https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-secrets-of-dead-cells-smart-constraints-unlocking-the-vault-3
- Verified: secondary (Game Developer summary of Benard's GDC 2019 talk)
- Review question: For each way the player can fail, is failure meant to happen there? If yes, what new thing does failure open?
- Use it for: MOTIVATION, MASTERY
- Limits: Dead Cells is a run-based game. In a persistent game, "death opens something new" needs another carrier, such as knowledge, unlocks, or a hub change.

### FromSoftware: hardship gives meaning, and aid is part of the design
- Claim: Hidetaka Miyazaki said that "in our games specifically, hardship is what gives meaning to the experience" and "It's our identity." He also said he feels "apologetic toward anyone who feels there's just too much to overcome." In 2024 he said that on his own Elden Ring run he used "everything I have at my disposal, all the assistance, every scrap of aid that the game offers." He said "the freedom and open-world nature of Elden Ring perhaps lowered the barrier to entry."
- Kind: principle
- Source: "Hidetaka Miyazaki Sees Death as a Feature, Not a Bug", Simon Parkin (The New Yorker), 2022, https://www.newyorker.com/culture/persons-of-interest/hidetaka-miyazaki-sees-death-as-a-feature-not-a-bug and "Pushing Buttons: meeting Hidetaka Miyazaki", Keza MacDonald (The Guardian), 2024, https://www.theguardian.com/games/article/2024/jun/26/pushing-buttons-meeting-hidetaka-miyazaki
- Verified: fetched primary (developer interviews in The New Yorker and The Guardian)
- Review question: When a player is stuck, what in-world aid exists (summons, other routes, other bosses, gear)? Does using it keep the sense of having overcome the challenge?
- Use it for: MASTERY, MOTIVATION, SOCIAL
- Limits: A statement of studio identity and one director's experience. It is not data on how many players use aid.

## System precedents: Solo companions

### Final Fantasy XIV Trust
- Claim: SAID (Naoki Yoshida, 2018): FFXIV "is an MMORPG first and foremost". Trust exists because "a subset of Final Fantasy fans ... have expressed their discomfort with trying XIV because it requires playing online with other people." ## Myths and misuses

Read this section before you cite a famous story. Several popular stories are wrong, overstated, or misattributed.

### From designer research

- "30 seconds of fun" does not mean "repeat one fun loop." Griesemer says people use it "to mean exactly the opposite of what I meant," and that the context must keep changing. Source: Engadget interview, July 14 2011, https://www.engadget.com/2011-07-14-half-minute-halo-an-interview-with-jaime-griesemer.html
- Meier's "series of interesting decisions" is quoted in several forms. Johnson's 2009 column gives it as "a good game is a series of interesting choices." The Escapist dates the first use to GDC 1989. In his 2012 talk, Meier says he does not claim it applies to every game (auto-captions, paraphrase). Do not use it as a universal definition. Sources: https://www.designer-notes.com/game-developer-column-5-sids-rules/ and https://www.escapistmagazine.com/gdc-2012-sid-meier-sees-interesting-decisions-even-in-rhythm-games/
- "Players will optimize the fun out of a game" is Soren Johnson's line (Game Developer, March 2011). TheGamer pairs it with "one of the responsibilities of designers is to protect the player from themselves" and credits that second line to Sid Meier, through a Game Maker's Toolkit video. That line does not appear in Johnson's column. Also, the column does not say "remove every exploit." It says "designers can go too far by trying to remove all exploits." Sources: https://www.designer-notes.com/game-developer-column-17-water-finds-a-crack/ and https://www.thegamer.com/players-optimize-fun-out-of-games/
- In MDA, "Aesthetics" means "the desirable emotional responses evoked in the player," not art style. Frank Lantz notes that people still "turn around and say 'the gameplay vs. the aesthetics.'" Sources: https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/MDA.pdf and https://gamedesignadvance.com/?p=2995
- "Double it or cut it in half" is a way to explore, not a claim about the right value. Johnson: "The point is not that the new values are likely to be correct." Source: https://www.designer-notes.com/game-developer-column-5-sids-rules/
- The Covert Action rule does not mean "never combine two kinds of play." The same column praises Pirates! and X-Com, where one part is the clear focus. Source: https://www.designer-notes.com/game-developer-column-5-sids-rules/
- Flow is not the same as automatic difficulty scaling. Chen argues that passive, performance-based DDA misreads players, and he prefers choices embedded in play. Source: https://www.jenovachen.com/flowingames/Flow_in_games_final.pdf
- Lazzaro's key names changed over time. The 2004 GDC material calls the third key Altered States (or the Internal Experience key) and the fourth Other People (or the Social Experience key). Her site now says Serious Fun and People Fun. Reports of the finding also differ: the talk text says hit games create emotion in "at least three of the Four Keys," but Game Developer's 2004 report says Halo and GTA 3 "appeared in all four categories." Cite the version you mean. Sources: https://www.nicolelazzaro.com/the4-keys-to-fun/ and https://www.gamedeveloper.com/marketing/postcard-from-gdc-2004-why-we-play-games-the-four-keys-to-player-experience
- "Balance" in Sawyer's sense is viability, not equal power. He says balance matters "to the extent that" it keeps different choices viable. Source: https://www.tumblr.com/jesawyer/161302725596/balance-in-single-player-crpgs
- Rosewater's lesson titles get shortened in secondhand notes. One notes page lists lesson 13 as "Make the fun part the correct strategy to win." The article title is "Make the fun part also the correct strategy to win." Quote the Making Magic articles, not summaries. Sources: https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/twenty-years-twenty-lessons-part-2-2016-06-06 and https://notes.hamatti.org/sources/talks/'magic-the-gathering'-20-years,-20-lessons-learned-by-mark-rosewater

- "Players destroyed Ultima Online's ecology by killing everything." Koster says no. It collapsed from a closed resource pool and player hoarding (players made huge numbers of shirts from wool), and the AI was cut for performance cost. It was removed during beta. - "The real-money auction house was Diablo 3's main problem." Wilson said the gold house did "much more damage" than the real-money house. Do not cite Diablo 3 as proof that real-money trade alone ruins loot. Source: Engadget 2013 (above).
- The WoW rested-XP story has no Blizzard primary source that I could find. The best chain is Shacknews's report of Pardo's GDC 2010 talk. - "Getting ganked makes new EVE players stay." - "Bartle's four player types are an empirical model." Yee writes that Bartle's assumptions "have never been empirically tested", and his data shows motives do not suppress each other. Source: Yee 2006 (above).
- "Old School RuneScape polls are the voice of the whole playerbase." Jagex says fewer than 6% of active members vote. Source: Jagex "Polling Changes", 2022 (above).
- "Toxicity comes from a few bad apples." Lin reports that the consistently toxic 1% produce about 5% of toxicity. Source: Game Developer 2015 (above).
- "Dungeon Finder killed WoW's server community" is a player claim (for example the MMO-Champion thread "Is the Dungeon Finder killing the WoW Community?", https://www.mmo-champion.com/threads/760717-Is-the-Dungeon-Finder-killing-the-WoW-Community). I found no Blizzard statement or data that confirms it. Pardo said the opposite in 2009, and Blizzard later added it to Wrath Classic after player feedback.
- "You think you do, but you don't" (J. Allen Brack, BlizzCon 2013) is often used as proof that developers misjudge demand. Wikipedia's World of Warcraft Classic article marks the claim as needing a citation, and I did not open a primary recording. Do not cite it without the recording. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_of_Warcraft_Classic
- "Lake of Kalandra had the worst retention in PoE history." This comes from community tracking, not from GGG. Wilson's post gives no retention numbers. Source: https://www.pathofexile.com/forum/view-thread/3300117
- "Mists of Pandaria dailies raised play time but felt like a chore." The chore half is a Blizzard statement. I found no source with play time data, so do not cite the play time half.
- EVE Online's in-house economist (hired 2007) is real, but the reports cover "inflation, economic growth and price trends". They measure the economy, not fun. Do not cite them as fun evidence. Source: "EVE Online Appoints In-World Economist", CCP Games, 2007, https://community.eveonline.com/news/news-channels/press-releases/eve-online-appoints-in-world-economist-1/

### From psychology research

#### Myth: Bartle's four types are validated, separate player personalities
- Reality: Bartle built them from a debate among MUD administrators. He warns readers not to "expect a conventionally rigorous approach". Yee's factor analysis found that motivations "do not suppress each other as Bartle suggested" (r < .10 among the main components).
- Source: Bartle 1996, https://mud.co.uk/richard/hcds.htm and Yee 2006, https://doi.org/10.1089/cpb.2006.9.772

#### Myth: Menus and option lists must have 7 plus or minus 2 items
- Reality: Nielsen: "It's a common misconception that limited short-term memory implies that menus should be similarly limited to 7 items." Menus rely on recognition, not recall. Cowan also shows that 7 was "a rough estimate and a rhetorical device".
- Source: Jakob Nielsen, "Short-Term Memory and Web Usability", NN/g, 2009, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/short-term-memory-and-web-usability/ and Cowan 2001, https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922

#### Myth: The jam study proves fewer choices are always better
- Reality: A meta-analysis of 63 conditions found "a mean effect size of virtually zero". Overload appears only under specific conditions: complex sets, hard tasks, uncertain preferences, and a goal to minimize effort.
- Source: Scheibehenne et al. 2010, https://doi.org/10.1086/651235 and Chernev et al. 2015, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002

#### Myth: Players remember unfinished tasks better (Zeigarnik), so cliffhangers and open loops stick in memory
- Reality: The 2025 meta-analysis "found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks". It did find a tendency to resume them (the Ovsiankina effect).
- Source: Ghibellini and Meier 2025, https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w

#### Myth: Losses always weigh about twice as much as gains
- Reality: The 1.955 mean hides the effect of study design. In studies where gains and losses were the same size and not ordered, the re-analysis found about 1.07. That is not significantly above 1.0.
- Source: Brown et al. 2024, https://doi.org/10.1257/jel.20221698 and Yechiam and Zeif 2025, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joep.2025.102801

#### Myth: Any reward kills intrinsic motivation (or the reverse: rewards are free motivation)
- Reality: In the 1999 meta-analysis, expected tangible rewards undermined free-choice motivation. Positive feedback enhanced it (d = 0.33). A rival review argues the harm is restricted and avoidable. In a controlled gamification test, points, levels, and leaderboards raised performance but did not change intrinsic motivation.
- Source: Deci, Koestner, Ryan 1999, https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627 and Eisenberger and Cameron 1996, https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.51.11.1153 and Mekler et al. 2017, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2015.08.048

#### Myth: Dopamine is the pleasure chemical, so more "dopamine hits" means more fun
- Reality: Berridge and Kringelbach report that the mesolimbic dopamine system "may not generate pleasure after all". More dopamine raises "wanting" (the drive to get a reward) but does not raise "liking". A reward loop that drives compulsive play does not prove that players enjoy it.
- Source: "Pleasure Systems in the Brain", Kent C. Berridge and Morten L. Kringelbach, 2015, Neuron, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.02.018 (fetched primary)

#### Myth: More juice is always better
- Reality: In a study of 3,018 players, extreme juiciness lowered play time, experience, motivation, and performance, as no juiciness did.
- Source: Kao 2020, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.entcom.2020.100359

#### Myth: Every system needs a tutorial, and locking the player down during it helps learning
- Reality: Tutorials helped only the most complex of three games. The authors found no evidence that restricting player freedom improves learnability.
- Source: Andersen et al. 2012, https://doi.org/10.1145/2207676.2207687

#### Myth: Asking players what they want, or why they did something, gives reliable design answers
- Reality: Valve's playtest slides list "People don't know why they do what they do" as a weakness of Q&A sessions. They stress the "Importance of what people do, not what they say".
- Source: Ambinder, GDC 2009, https://cdn.akamai.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2009/GDC2009_ValvesApproachToPlaytesting.pdf

#### Myth: Peak-end means only the peak and the end matter, so the middle can be dull
- Reality: The meta-analysis found the effect of the overall average was "comparable" to the peak-end effect. The effect of duration was "essentially nil", but the average of all moments counted.
- Source: Alaybek et al. 2022, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2022.104149

#### Myth: Failure and death always hurt the player experience, so difficulty must always match skill
- Reality: Dark Souls III players reported mostly positive experiences. Those experiences were "enabled by negative events such as difficulties and avatar death".
- Source: Petralito et al. 2017, https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3026047

#### Myth: More button presses and busier play make a game more immersive
- Reality: More physical interaction alone did not raise immersion. Time pressure with cognitive challenge did.
- Source: Cox et al. 2012, https://doi.org/10.1145/2207676.2207689

#### Misuse: Treating the loot box and gambling link as proof in either direction
- Reality: The link replicates (eta squared 0.054 and 0.051, mean r = .27 across studies). But the authors say the data cannot show which way the cause runs. Do not claim loot boxes are proven to cause gambling. Do not claim they are proven harmless.
- Source: Zendle and Cairns 2018, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0206767 and 2019, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0213194 and Spicer et al. 2022, https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448211027175

#### Misuse: Treating the endowment effect as a fixed law of ownership
- Reality: Plott and Zeiler trained subjects on the valuation procedure and then saw no gap for mugs. Isoni et al. still found a gap for lotteries. The effect depends on the good and the procedure.
- Source: Plott and Zeiler 2005, https://doi.org/10.1257/0002828054201387 and Isoni, Loomes, Sugden 2011, https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.101.2.991

### From system precedents

#### "More item combinations make better loot" (Borderlands' "87 bazillion guns")
- Myth: The Borderlands marketing number shows that huge procedural variety is the goal.
- What the source shows: At SXSW 2010, Gearbox developers counted 16,164,886 guns. Lead designer Matthew Armstrong said: "I want you to be attached to your gun. A lot of people who have played the game multiple times think that's a flaw, but then I point out that they've played the game multiple times."
- Source: "SXSW: Creating 87 bazillion guns for Borderlands", Kevin Kelly (Engadget), 2010, https://www.engadget.com/2010-03-16-sxsw-creating-87-bazillion-guns-for-borderlands.html
- Verified: secondary (Engadget)

#### "The real-money auction house alone sank Diablo III"
- Myth: Remove the auction house and the launch game was fine.
- What the source shows: Mosqueira named at least two more causes. Loot was scarce ("104 hours before I found my first legendary"). Difficulty pushed players to farm pots instead of monsters.
- Source: PCGamesN GDC 2015 report, 2015, https://www.pcgamesn.com/diablo-iii/diablo-3s-josh-mosqueria-on-launch-failures-mistakes-and-the-road-to-redemption
- Verified: secondary (PCGamesN)

#### "Players optimize the fun out, so the players are to blame"
- Misuse: Citing Soren Johnson to excuse a degenerate strategy.
- What the source shows: Johnson's examples end in designer fixes (per-city costs in Civ 4) or player options. He wrote that "a single, dominant strategy actually takes away choice" and that "the designer can't go wrong putting the player in control of his or her own experience."
- Source: GD Column 17: Water Finds a Crack, Soren Johnson, 2011, https://www.designer-notes.com/game-developer-column-17-water-finds-a-crack/
- Verified: fetched primary

#### "FromSoftware design means refusing to help the player"
- Myth: A "Soulslike" should not offer aid, or aid spoils the design.
- What the source shows: Miyazaki said he used "every scrap of aid that the game offers" and credited the open world with lowering "the barrier to entry."
- Source: "Pushing Buttons: meeting Hidetaka Miyazaki", Keza MacDonald (The Guardian), 2024, https://www.theguardian.com/games/article/2024/jun/26/pushing-buttons-meeting-hidetaka-miyazaki
- Verified: fetched primary

#### "Players hate raids, so cut raids"
- Misuse: Treating complaints about one raid rule as a verdict on raids.
- What the sources show: Valheim players complained when monsters ran past them to wreck buildings. Iron Gate fixed the targeting and later added raid settings. It did not remove raids. RimWorld's fix was to make wall-breaching raids uncommon (from about 90% to about 33% for late mechanoids) so defenses matter again.
- Source: Valheim Patch 0.156.2, Iron Gate, 2021, https://steamcommunity.com/ogg/892970/announcements/detail/2998816540608254733 and RimWorld Update 1.3.3200, Ludeon Studios, 2021, https://steamcommunity.com/ogg/294100/announcements/detail/3137316801198575090
- Verified: fetched primary

#### "A hard mode means enemies with more health"
- Misuse: Making difficulty by scaling enemy HP only.
- What the source shows: About Fallout 4's Survival mode, Todd Howard said: "We're not trying to make it a ton harder. It's harder because you're doing more things. We want the combat to feel different, as opposed to just being a bullet sponge."
- Source: "Todd Howard Discusses Fallout 4 DLC, Mods, & Survival Mode", Andrew Reiner (Game Informer), 2016, https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2016/02/22/todd-howard-discusses-fallout-4-dlc-mods-amp-survival-mode.aspx
- Verified: fetched primary (developer interview in Game Informer)

#### "Sailing proved players strongly want new skills"
- Myth: Old School RuneScape's Sailing vote was a landslide.
- What the source shows: Jagex said the Sailing vote started at 76% and stayed "around 71-2%." It passed under the 70% threshold set in 2022. Under the old 75% threshold it would likely have failed. Warding failed in 2019 at 66.4%.
- Source: Sailing Poll Results Summary, Jagex, 2023, https://secure.runescape.com/m=news/sailing-poll-results-summary?oldschool=1 and Polling Changes, Jagex, 2022, https://secure.runescape.com/m=news/polling-changes?oldschool=1
- Verified: fetched primary

#### "Factorio measured that players quit before automation"
- Myth: Friday Facts #241 has drop-off data.
- What the source shows: The post gives the tutorial length ("about 30-45 minutes to get to automation") and the team's diagnosis. It gives no player drop-off numbers. Search-engine summaries during this research added a "30 minutes then quit" claim that the post does not contain.
- Source: Friday Facts #241, Wube Software, 2018, https://www.factorio.com/blog/post/fff-241
- Verified: fetched primary

#### Misattribution: the Duriel frost Sorceress example
- Myth: Jay Wilson used a Diablo II frost Sorceress vs. Duriel story to justify removing skill points.
- What the source shows: The Blizzplanet page has the Wilson quote. The Duriel example comes from the page's own author, not from Wilson. Search summaries during this research attributed it to Wilson.
- Source: IGN: Blizzard On Ditching Skill Points in Diablo III, Blizzplanet, 2011, https://diablo.blizzplanet.com/blog/comments/ign-blizzard-on-ditching-skill-points-in-diablo-iii
- Verified: secondary (Blizzplanet)
